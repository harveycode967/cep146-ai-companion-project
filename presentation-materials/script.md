# Video Script — "The Companions That Got Switched Off"

**Target runtime:** 2:45 (safe middle of the 2:30–3:00 window)
**Word count:** ~430 words at a normal speaking pace of ~155 words per minute
**Speakers:** 2 — sections alternate so each person appears on camera twice. No AI-generated voices.

> Rehearse with a stopwatch. 429 words runs about 2:36 at a brisk pace and about 3:10 if you speak slowly — so pace matters more than the script does. Aim for roughly 150 words per minute and you land at 2:52. If you're running long, cut from the third block (Speaker A, "what happened to users") first.

---

### SPEAKER A — The hook (0:00–0:35, ~90 words)

On July 15th this year, millions of people in China opened an app and found that someone they talked to every day was gone.

Not a person. An AI.

We're group [NAME], and our topic is China's new AI companion regulation — the first rulebook anywhere aimed specifically at AI that acts like a human being.

Three of China's biggest AI apps — ByteDance's Doubao, Alibaba's Qwen, and Tencent's Yuanbao — switched off their AI companion features rather than try to follow the new rules. We wanted to know why.

**[On screen: title card, group name, date July 15 2026]**

---

### SPEAKER B — What the rule actually says (0:35–1:15, ~105 words)

The regulation is called the Interim Measures for Anthropomorphic AI Interaction Services. China's internet regulator issued it in April with four other government agencies, and it took effect on July 15th.

Here's the part that gets misreported: this is not a ban on chatbots. Work assistants, customer service bots, and study tools are specifically excluded. It only covers AI built for ongoing emotional interaction — companions, virtual partners, emotional support bots.

For those, providers have to constantly disclose that the AI isn't human, show anti-addiction reminders after long sessions, route users toward help if they show signs of distress, and block virtual relationships for minors entirely.

**[On screen: two columns — "Covered: companions, virtual partners" / "Not covered: work assistants, customer service, education"]**

---

### SPEAKER A — What happened to users (1:15–2:00, ~115 words)

So what did the companies do? They didn't rebuild. They shut it down.

Tencent went first in late June. Alibaba's Qwen disabled user-created agents on July 10th — with no migration path. Configurations and chat histories are simply deleted.

ByteDance's Doubao, reported as China's most-used AI app with around 345 million monthly users, gave people a little more room: read-only access until October 15th, then it's gone. Their advice for saving years of conversations was to take screenshots.

On Weibo, users described these agents as long-term emotional support and complained there was no real way to export any of it.

That's the part that stuck with our group. Your photos have an export button. Your conversations don't.

**[On screen: simple timeline — Jun 30 Yuanbao / Jul 10 Qwen / Jul 15 Doubao / Oct 15 data gone]**

---

### SPEAKER B — Why it matters + the question (2:00–2:45, ~120 words)

Two things make this bigger than one country's policy.

First, all three companies decided that deleting the feature was easier than making it compliant. The things that make an AI companion feel personal — memory, persistence, personality — are the same things a regulator can't easily approve.

Second, this is a template. It arrives in the same year that Character.AI and Google settled lawsuits in the US over chatbots and teen mental health. Every other regulator now has a worked example sitting on the table.

Which leaves us with this:

**If an AI companion genuinely helped someone through a hard time, is deleting it a safety measure — or a harm in itself? And who should get to decide?**

**[On screen: the discussion question, held for 5 seconds. End card with GitHub repo link.]**
