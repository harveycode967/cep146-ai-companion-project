# Research Brief — China's AI Companion Regulation

**Topic:** China's Interim Measures for the Administration of Anthropomorphic AI Interaction Services, and the shutdown of AI companion features on Doubao, Qwen, and Yuanbao.

**Why this qualifies as a current event:** The regulation was issued 10 April 2026 and took effect 15 July 2026 — six days ago at time of writing. Well inside the 6–12 month window.

---

## 1. What the regulation is

Issued 10 April 2026 by the Cyberspace Administration of China (CAC) together with four other agencies: the National Development and Reform Commission, the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology, the Ministry of Public Security, and the State Administration for Market Regulation. Took effect 15 July 2026 after a grace period.

It is described by multiple law-firm analyses as China's first regulation aimed specifically at AI that simulates human personality and provides ongoing emotional interaction. It sits on top of China's earlier AI rules — the algorithmic recommendation provisions (2022), deep synthesis provisions (2022), and the generative AI interim measures (2023).

**Scope — this is the detail most people get wrong.** The rules apply to services that simulate a natural person's personality traits, thinking patterns, and communication style in order to provide *continuous emotional interaction* — companionship, emotional support, virtual relationships. They explicitly exclude non-emotional services: customer service bots, knowledge Q&A, work assistants, education, and research tools.

So it is **not** a ban on AI chatbots. It is a ban on a specific product category. Do not let the video say "China banned AI companions" without that qualifier — that's the kind of inaccuracy the rubric penalises under "Is the information accurate?"

## 2. What the rules actually require

Drawn from law-firm summaries (Bird & Bird, Hunton Andrews Kurth, Licentium) of the Chinese-language text:

- Providers must continuously and prominently disclose that the service is AI, not a human.
- Services must not have as their goal replacing real social interaction, controlling users' psychology, or inducing addiction or dependence.
- Anti-addiction reminders for sessions running past roughly two hours, and "emotional boundary" guidance.
- Tiered crisis-intervention pathways where a user shows signs of self-harm or acute distress.
- Minors: virtual intimate-relationship services (virtual partners, virtual family members) are prohibited for minors outright. Under-14 users require parent or guardian consent. Providers must detect minors and switch automatically into a "minor mode" with time limits, reminders to return to real-world interaction, and parental controls.
- Elderly users are also named as a protected group.
- Algorithm filing with the CAC and a security assessment required.
- No training on user data without consent.

## 3. What happened to the platforms

Rather than rebuild their products to comply, three of China's largest consumer AI apps withdrew the features:

- **Tencent's Yuanbao** — pulled its equivalent feature first, in late June 2026.
- **Alibaba's Qwen** — disabled user-created agents on 10 July, full shutdown by 15 July. No grace period and no migration path announced; agent configurations and chat histories are permanently deleted.
- **ByteDance's Doubao** — agent features went offline 15 July. Doubao cited "product function adjustments." Users keep **read-only** access to configurations and chat histories until **15 October 2026**, after which the data is handled under the standard privacy policy and is no longer recoverable in the app. ByteDance advised users to export via screenshots or text sharing, and pointed them to a separate app for building new agents.

Scale: Doubao is reported as China's most-used AI app at roughly 345 million monthly active users. Note that figure covers the whole app, not only the agent feature — don't claim 345 million people lost companions.

Enforcement was not theoretical. Shanghai's cyberspace authority removed over 14,000 non-compliant agents in a sweep on 26 June, ahead of the deadline, targeting impersonation bots and privacy violations.

There was visible public reaction — Weibo users described the agents as long-running emotional support and complained there was no easy way to export chat histories.

## 4. Why it matters to society (this is a graded criterion)

Three angles, pick one or two — don't try to cover all three in three minutes:

**a) Emotional data has no legal home.** Your photos have export tools. Years of conversations with an AI companion don't. When the product is withdrawn, the relationship and its entire history go with it. Doubao's three-month read-only window and Qwen's zero-day cutoff are the same problem at two different speeds.

**b) Compliance was harder than deletion.** All three companies decided that rebuilding was less practical than switching the feature off. That says something about how deeply persistent memory and emotional personalisation are wired into these products — the features that make a companion feel personal are the same ones that make it hard to regulate.

**c) It's a template.** This is one of the first concrete rulebooks anywhere for human–AI emotional relationships, and it lands in the same period as lawsuits in the US over chatbots and teen mental health — Character.AI and Google settled such lawsuits in January 2026. Other regulators now have a worked example to copy or reject.

2. If an AI companion helped someone through a hard time, is deleting it a safety measure or a harm in itself — and who should get to decide?

4. Should protections like minor modes and session limits apply to AI assistants everywhere, or only to ones designed for emotional relationships?
